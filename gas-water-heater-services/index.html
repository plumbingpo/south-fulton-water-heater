Most gas water heaters in South Fulton, GA work the same basic way. Gas enters through a control valve that doubles as the thermostat and feeds a burner beneath the tank, and the hot exhaust travels up a flue through the middle of the tank and out through the vent. A thermocouple, or a flame sensor on newer electronic models, tells the valve a flame is present; without that signal, the valve closes for safety. The design is dependable, but it has a few predictable weak spots. Thermocouples wear out, pilot tubes and burner ports clog, the flame arrestor screen at the base packs up with lint and dust, control valves fail, and vents lose their draft.

Diagnosing a gas water heater properly means checking combustion, not just parts. Across South Fulton, our technicians measure thermocouple output in millivolts, study the pilot and burner flame for color and shape, read gas pressure at the valve, and test draft at the hood so we know exhaust is leaving the house. We check the combustion chamber for soot and scorching, which point to a blocked flue or poor combustion air, and we test for carbon monoxide near the heater. Safety always comes first: a gas smell means leaving the house and calling the gas utility from outside before anything else, and South Fulton Water Heater can take over from there.

Symptoms That Mean It's Time to Call

Water heaters usually give plenty of warning before they fail, as long as you know what to look and listen for. If any of the following sound familiar, it's worth having the unit checked:

  • Hot water that runs out faster than it used to
  • A silent heater with no flame and no hot water
  • Popping, rumbling, or crackling sounds as the burner heats
  • Water pooling around the base of the heater
  • Any smell of gas around the appliance, which calls for leaving and phoning the gas utility
  • A pilot that lights, then goes out when you release the knob
  • Exhaust odor, rust, or moisture around the draft hood and vent pipe
  • A flame that looks orange or floppy rather than steady and blue

Why This Job Calls for a Pro

Gas water heaters are among the riskiest appliances to work on without training, largely because you cannot see the biggest hazards. A gas connection that is not quite tight can leak slowly for days. A vent connector that has lost its slope, or a flue partly blocked by debris, can let carbon monoxide spill into the house every time the burner fires, with no smell to warn you. Gas valve and heater replacements also typically require a permit and an inspection. Relighting a pilot by following the label is something most homeowners can do; almost everything past that deserves a technician.

The value of calling a pro is certainty. We test instead of guessing, from thermocouple millivolt readings and burner flame checks to gas pressure at the valve and a draft test at the hood. We leak-test every connection, confirm the vent is carrying exhaust out of the house, and check CO before we leave. And because some gas heaters are not worth another repair, we give you a straight answer on repair versus replacement first, then handle either one with correct venting, gas piping, and the code items your installation requires.

Prevention Tips That Actually Help

Routine maintenance is almost always cheaper and easier than an early replacement. Start with these practical measures our team relies on:

  • Check that the vent connector is secure and rising toward the chimney or flue
  • Clean dust from around the base so the burner gets plenty of combustion air
  • Make sure your home has CO alarms that work, especially with gas appliances
  • Flush the tank on a regular schedule so sediment never bakes onto the bottom
  • Check the anode rod every three to five years, since it protects the tank from corrosion
  • Keep the thermostat around 120 degrees to save energy and reduce scalding risk
  • Never store flammable liquids near a gas water heater
